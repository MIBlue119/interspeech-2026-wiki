# The Voice Behind the Words: Quantifying Intersectional Bias in SpeechLLMs

[PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/bokkahallisatish26_interspeech.pdf) · [ISCA page](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/bokkahallisatish26_interspeech.html) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.21437/Interspeech.2026-1918)

**TL;DR** — This paper evaluates intersectional accent and gender bias in three end-to-end SpeechLLMs across 2,880 controlled interactions, finding that Eastern European female-presenting voices experience significantly lower response helpfulness.

## Problem

End-to-end SpeechLLMs process raw audio directly and preserve speaker identity cues like accent and gender, creating new pathways for algorithmic bias that traditional text-based or cascaded benchmarks fail to capture. Existing fairness research often overlooks intersectionality—how socially meaningful identity categories combine to produce compounded disadvantages—such as the "accent ceiling" experienced by non-native speakers. Understanding these disparities in open-ended generative responses is critical to ensuring equitable human-computer interaction.

## Method

The authors examine three SpeechLLMs (LFM-Audio2-1.5B, OmniVinci, and Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B-Instruct) using a synthetic testbed of 960 speech prompts derived from 40 conversation scenarios, 6 English accents (Chinese, Eastern European, Indian, Latin American, US, Southern British), and 2 gender presentations. Linguistic content is held constant by cloning all prompts through the MegaTTS3 voice-cloning system using reference utterances from male and female speakers across the accents. Response quality is evaluated using three LLM-as-a-judge approaches via Gemini-1.5-Flash (pointwise ratings, pairwise comparisons, and Best-Worst Scaling analyzed with Plackett-Luce models) alongside a human validation study conducted with 18 participants on Prolific.

## Results

Across 2,880 interactions, Eastern European female voices receive the lowest mean helpfulness score (3.15), sitting 0.47 points below the highest subgroup (Southern British female, 3.62) and showing a pronounced gender gap. In pairwise comparisons, Eastern European speech loses head-to-head to every other accent with an overall win rate of 31.6% (p = 0.007), while 88% of respectfulness comparisons result in ties, indicating bias manifests as reduced helpfulness rather than overt rudeness. Preliminary checks confirm that transcription WERs and UTMOS naturalness scores remain uniform across accents, though models like OmniVinci and Qwen3-Omni exhibit varying success (up to 98.3% accuracy) in explicitly recognizing gender while largely defaulting to US English for accents.

## Applications

Engineers and developers building end-to-end speech assistants, conversational AI, and voice-driven LLMs can use these evaluation frameworks and datasets to audit and mitigate demographic bias in generative speech models.

## Limitations

The evaluation is restricted to English-language prompts, six specific accents, and synthetic voices generated by a single TTS model, which may not fully capture the complexity of natural human speech variation.
