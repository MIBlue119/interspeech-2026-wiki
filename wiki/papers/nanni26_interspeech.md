# Rethinking Consent Acquisition for Voice Synthesis: from Static to Dynamic Consent

[PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/nanni26_interspeech.pdf) · [ISCA page](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/nanni26_interspeech.html) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.21437/Interspeech.2026-2379)

**TL;DR** — This paper critically evaluates current contractual models for voice synthesis consent and proposes adopting dynamic consent from bioethics to better protect identity-based rights.

## Problem

Voice cloning technologies treat human voices purely as data assets subject to one-time authorization, ignoring the reality that voices act as intrinsic markers of personal identity. Standard general and specific contracts fail to satisfy informed consent requirements because users cannot anticipate future uses or harms. Consequently, voice talent and speakers frequently face unauthorized exploitation and identity-based harm without ongoing agency or control.

## Method

The paper analyzes the philosophical and legal tensions between the voice as a commodity versus an intrinsic identity property. It examines concrete industry examples of general consent contracts (such as BeyondWords' terms granting broad intellectual property rights) and specific consent agreements. By drawing parallels to bioethics, the paper introduces dynamic consent as an alternative governance framework. This approach shifts focus from static data acquisition to continuous, ongoing control and communication between voice providers and AI operators throughout the technology's lifecycle.

## Results

The study qualitatively reviews real-world case studies, such as actor Paul Skye Lehrman discovering his voice used without permission in a podcast, and contractual clauses from commercial voice generation platforms. It demonstrates how current general consent agreements legally strip contributors of moral and intellectual property rights while leaving subsequent uses unregulated. The analysis shows that neither broad nor narrow static contracts satisfy the criteria of informed, voluntary, and capacitated decision-making. The proposed dynamic consent framework is shown to re-establish speaker agency, though its primary limitation is that it may currently require significant negotiating power from the voice provider.

## Applications

Speech engineers, legal scholars, and policymakers designing ethical frameworks, consent workflows, and governance models for text-to-speech and voice cloning technologies.

## Limitations

Dynamic consent approaches are practically limited because only individuals with substantial negotiating leverage can effectively demand ongoing control and custom terms from voice synthesis companies.
