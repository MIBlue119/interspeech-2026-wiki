# Cross-Attention is Half Explanation in Speech-to-Text Models

[PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/papi26_interspeech.pdf) · [ISCA page](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/papi26_interspeech.html) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.21437/Interspeech.2026-40)

**TL;DR** — This paper evaluates whether cross-attention can serve as a lightweight explanation proxy for speech-to-text models, finding that it accounts for roughly 50% of input feature relevance and 52-75% of encoder output saliency.

## Problem

Cross-attention scores in speech-to-text models are widely used for downstream tasks like timestamp estimation and alignment under the assumption that they reliably indicate input-output dependencies. However, this assumption has never been formally verified in the speech domain, and context mixing within the encoder may obscure true input alignment. Because perturbation-based feature attribution methods are computationally expensive, it is critical to determine whether lightweight cross-attention scores can faithfully substitute for formal explainability techniques.

## Method

The study systematically compares cross-attention (CA) scores with input saliency maps (SMX) generated by SPES, a state-of-the-art feature-attribution method for autoregressive speech models, and encoder-output saliency maps (SMH) to isolate context mixing effects. The analysis encompasses automatic speech recognition (ASR) and speech translation (ST) tasks across monolingual, multilingual, and multitask settings using state-of-the-art speech processing architectures at multiple scales. CA matrices are frame-wise mean-variance normalized to handle attention sinks, while SMX and SMH are normalized across tokens, and Pearson correlation coefficients are computed on flattened representations to measure alignment.

## Results

Evaluating across various speech architectures, tasks, and scales, the authors discover that cross-attention exhibits moderate correlations with input saliency maps but only accounts for about 50% of input relevance. When compared against encoder output representations (SMH), cross-attention achieves at best 52-75% alignment, revealing that context mixing significantly alters how the decoder accesses encoded features. Aggregating attention scores across layers and heads yields more stable patterns, but local token-specific interactions remain poorly captured by raw attention weights.

## Limitations
