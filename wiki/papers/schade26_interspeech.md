# Naming Heroes and Villains – The Influence of Phonaesthetics

[PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/schade26_interspeech.pdf) · [ISCA page](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/schade26_interspeech.html) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.21437/Interspeech.2026-2247)

**TL;DR** — This study investigates whether lay speakers unconsciously apply phonaesthetic sound symbolism when naming fictional fantasy heroes and villains, revealing a significant preference for higher-sonority syllables in hero names and lower-sonority syllables in villain names.

## Problem

While constructed languages like Tolkien's Orkish or Elvish exploit phonaesthetics to evoke character traits, it remains unclear how universally lay speakers generalize these sound-symbolic design principles when inventing names. Understanding whether phonological dimensions like sonority, vowel length, and syllable complexity systematically map onto moral evaluations provides insight into human linguistic creativity and sound symbolism. This gap is tested experimentally using controlled stimulus inventories.

## Method

32 native German speakers participated in 16 dyads, completing an interactive browser-based naming task where they selected two syllable building blocks from a 2x2 matrix to name AI-generated hero and villain character images. The syllable inventory systematically varied sonority index (high vs. low), nucleus length (short V vs. long VV/diphthong), and CV-complexity. Data were analyzed using Generalized Linear Mixed Effects Models (GLMERs) with character type, gender, and interacting phonaesthetic features as fixed effects.

## Results

Across 96 trials (192 selected syllables), syllable bin distributions differed significantly between heroes and villains (Chi-square p < 0.001), confirming a strong sonority effect. Heroes favored high-sonority short-vowel syllables (hi.V at 40.62%), whereas villains favored low-sonority short-vowel syllables (lo.V at 36.45%). GLMER analyses revealed significant main effects of character type (hero vs. villain) and the sonority of adjacent selections on syllable choice (p < 0.003 and p < 0.001), while estimated marginal means showed no significant differences across character genders.

## Applications

Speech engineers, game developers, and narrative designers studying character naming conventions, or researchers investigating sound symbolism and cross-linguistic phonaesthetics.

## Limitations

The study tested only native German speakers and focused solely on initial syllable selection rather than the final co-constructed spoken names.
