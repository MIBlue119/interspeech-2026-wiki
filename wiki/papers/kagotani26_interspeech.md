# Effects of distributional bias in vowels and consonants on the Speech-to-Song Illusion

[PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/kagotani26_interspeech.pdf) · [ISCA page](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/kagotani26_interspeech.html) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.21437/Interspeech.2026-2956)

**TL;DR** — Manipulating phonological distributional bias and instructions toward rap significantly enhances the perceived strength of the Speech-to-Song illusion.

## Problem

Prior research on the Speech-to-Song (STS) illusion has focused heavily on prosodic and semantic features, leaving the impact of phonological distributional structures like vowel and consonant frequencies unexamined. Understanding whether these distributional biases drive musicalization helps clarify the perceptual boundaries between speech, poetry, and music.

## Method

The authors conducted a listening experiment with 120 native Japanese speakers evaluating 40 short Japanese phrases (~9 morae each) recorded by a professional male announcer. Stimuli were built across a 2x2 design varying vowel bias and consonant bias using Shannon entropy to quantify phonemic concentration. Participants rated phrases before and after ten repetitions across two instruction blocks: Song-likeness and Rap-likeness.

## Results

A three-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed significant main effects for Vowel Bias, Consonant Bias, and Instruction, alongside a significant two-way interaction between Vowel and Consonant Bias. Biased stimuli elicited significantly larger perceptual shifts than non-biased counterparts (e.g., mean differences of -0.28, -0.23, and -0.35 against the non-bias condition, all p < .001). The Rap instruction consistently yielded higher ratings of musicalization than the Song instruction (mean change 0.95 vs 0.84, F(1,119) = 6.20, p = .014).

## Applications

Speech and audio researchers studying perceptual audio illusions, cognitive psychology, and the structural design of musical or poetic speech forms.

## Limitations

The study utilized a binary manipulation of distributional bias and tested exclusively Japanese phrases with native speakers, noting that natural pitch fluctuations in speech hindered traditional song-likeness frameworks.
