# Not all language switching is equal: Language brokering and code-switching are associated with working memory and inhibitory control in young adults

[PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/wright26_interspeech.pdf) · [ISCA page](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/wright26_interspeech.html) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.21437/Interspeech.2026-3404)

**TL;DR** — This study investigates how distinct types of bilingual language use relate to executive functions in young adults, revealing that communicative function and motivation matter more than raw code-switching frequency.

## Problem

Empirical links between bilingual code-switching and executive function (EF) have been mixed, partly because studies often treat bilingualism as a unitary experience and rely on noisy single-task EF measures. Understanding whether bilingual speech shapes cognitive control requires accounting for the heterogeneity of everyday speaking practices—such as conversational switching, lexical retrieval difficulties, and informal language brokering—using sensitive latent variable models.

## Method

The study evaluates 697 young adult bilingual undergraduate students, utilizing a filtered subset of 176 complete cases for covariate-adjusted regressions. Executive functions are modeled as three correlated latent variables (shifting, updating, and response inhibition) derived from a confirmatory factor analysis of nine behavioral tasks. Language experience metrics include overall conversational code-switching frequency, exploratory factor analysis (EFA)-derived motivations for switching (identity, enjoyment, pragmatic, and lexical), and frequency of informal language brokering. Linear regression models control for age, language dominance, overall oral proficiency, and socioeconomic status.

## Results

Overall conversational code-switching frequency showed no significant association with shifting, updating, or response inhibition (all ps > .30). In contrast, lexically motivated switching showed positive associations with updating and response inhibition (both ps = .007), whereas frequent language brokering was negatively associated with updating (p < .001) and response inhibition (p = .007). Shifting was not significantly predicted by any language-use measures. In the comprehensive multi-predictor model, these dissociations between lexical motivation and language brokering remained robust while accounting for up to 17.4% of the variance.

## Applications

## Limitations

The cross-sectional design prevents causal claims, and everyday code-switching and language brokering frequencies relied on single-item self-report measures.
