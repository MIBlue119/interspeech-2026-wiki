# Positioning Effect of Syllable Shortening on Speech Segmentation: Evidence from Mandarin Listeners

[PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/ou26_interspeech.pdf) · [ISCA page](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/ou26_interspeech.html) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.21437/Interspeech.2026-234)

**TL;DR** — This study investigates how syllable shortening impacts speech segmentation in Mandarin listeners, demonstrating that middle-syllable reduction facilitates word identification while final-syllable shortening hinders it.

## Problem

While prominent prosodic cues like lengthening often signal word boundaries, the role of prosodic reduction such as syllable shortening in speech segmentation remains understudied. Understanding whether listeners utilize durational reduction as a structural cue helps clarify how human auditory systems chunk continuous speech.

## Method

An artificial language learning experiment was conducted with 120 native Mandarin participants split across four conditions: a baseline transitional probability (TP) only condition, initial shortening, middle shortening, and final shortening. Stimuli consisted of trisyllabic nonsense words where target syllables were durationally manipulated from 300 ms down to 150 ms using Praat. Participants listened to a 11-12 minute continuous speech stream and completed a 36-trial two-alternative forced-choice test comparing words to part-words.

## Results

Mean segmentation accuracies were 55.56% for the TP baseline, 55.46% for initial shortening, 65.28% for middle shortening, and 52.87% for final shortening. Mixed-effects logistic regression indicated that middle-syllable shortening significantly facilitated segmentation compared to the control (z = 2.29, p = 0.0217), whereas final-syllable shortening significantly impaired it (z = -2.64, p = 0.0084). Initial shortening showed no statistically significant effect relative to baseline.

## Applications

## Limitations

The study is limited to native Mandarin listeners evaluating trisyllabic nonsense words, leaving cross-linguistic generalizability and the interaction with other cues like pitch for future work.
