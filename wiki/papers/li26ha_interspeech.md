# Phonetic evidence for contrastive voicing in Nakanamanga coronal plosives

[PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/li26ha_interspeech.pdf) · [ISCA page](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/li26ha_interspeech.html) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.21437/Interspeech.2026-2832)

**TL;DR** — This study provides the first robust acoustic evidence supporting a two-way phonemic contrast between a voiceless dental plosive and a voiced prenasalised postalveolar plosive in the Tongoa variety of Nakanamanga.

## Problem

For the understudied Oceanic language Nakanamanga, linguists have long debated whether there are one or two coronal plosive phonemes, which complicates historical reconstructions and regional language mapping. Previous descriptive studies were largely inconclusive or limited in scope, and reliable acoustic measurements were entirely lacking. Resolving this issue clarifies the phonological inventory and illuminates broader patterns of plosive retention and devoicing in central Vanuatu.

## Method

The investigation utilized a wordlist of 12 disyllabic items featuring target word-medial plosives following short or long /ɔ/. Speech data were gathered from eight native speakers on Tongoa Island and in Port Vila using a Zoom H6 recorder and RØDE NT3 microphone at 96 kHz/24-bit. Recordings comprised five consecutive repetitions within a standard carrier phrase, yielding 443 analyzed tokens after filtering. Manual segmentation in Praat established voice onset time (VOT) and closure durations, while intensity cues isolated nasal versus oral closure intervals for prenasalised stops. Linear mixed-effects models tested the effects of phoneme type, syllable count, and preceding vowel length on VOT and closure duration, controlling for speaker and word random intercepts.

## Results

Across 443 tokens, the voiceless dental plosive showed a consistent positive VOT (mean = 29 ms, SD = 14) and shorter closure duration (mean = 80 ms, SD = 18). Conversely, the voiced prenasalised postalveolar plosive demonstrated reliable prevoicing with negative VOT (mean = -98 ms, SD = 24) and longer overall closure (mean = 98 ms, SD = 24), where the nasal component accounted for roughly 65% of the total closure interval. Linear mixed-effects modeling confirmed that phoneme category exerts a highly significant effect on both VOT (p < 0.001, with an estimated 124.88 ms difference) and closure duration (p < 0.001), validating the distinct phonemic statuses of /t″/ and /n̪d/ across speakers and lexical items.
