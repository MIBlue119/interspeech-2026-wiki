# Should Robots Sound more like Machines than like Humans? User Expectations Affect the Perception of Prosody in TTS Voices

[PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/shim26_interspeech.pdf) · [ISCA page](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/shim26_interspeech.html) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.21437/Interspeech.2026-3075)

**TL;DR** — Manipulating indexical voice quality to make a neural TTS robot voice sound more machine-like unexpectedly improves listener comprehension of syntactically ambiguous sentences containing prosodic boundaries (commas), whereas altering pitch for affective expression affects perceived naturalness but not intelligibility.

## Problem

Current text-to-speech (TTS) research treats intelligibility as separate from extra-linguistic prosodic and indexical cues, focusing primarily on qualitative subjective metrics like naturalness and MOS. However, listeners frequently struggle to perceive subtle prosodic boundaries in synthesized speech that are crucial for resolving syntactic ambiguities. Understanding how these voice design choices influence actual language comprehension remains a critical gap in human-robot interaction.

## Method

The study evaluates four voice conditions synthesized using a fine-tuned Matcha-TTS model: Human-like Affective, Human-like Monotonic, Machine-like Affective, and Machine-like Monotonic. Fine-tuning used recordings from a female native English speaker reading prosodically contrasted sentences. Monotonic variants were generated by reducing pitch height and variation by 40% via the Praat Vocal Toolkit, while machine-like variants applied a specific robotic distortion command (Delay 0.02s with an Amplitude of 0.5 Pa). 160 native English-speaking participants (ages 19–30) completed a robot training avatar task (picture-matching validation of 40 unique sentences comprising direct address, lists, and vowel-tense fillers) followed by an 11-point scale MOS-X2 and Godspeed questionnaire.

## Results

Evaluated on 160 participants using mixed-effects logistic regression and cumulative link models across 40 trials per participant. Pitch manipulation (affective vs. monotonic) significantly increased ratings of naturalness (p < .01) but did not impact intelligibility. Conversely, machine-like indexical voice quality significantly increased intelligibility accuracy specifically for sentences with commas (three-way interaction p = .019; human vs. machine contrast z = -2.29, p = .022), counter to subjective questionnaire ratings where machine-like voices were marginally rated as less intelligent (p = .064). Questionnaire items showed no main effect of Voice Condition on overall composite scores (p > .10).

## Applications

Speech engineers and human-computer interaction designers building conversational agents or robot avatars that need to reliably convey syntactically complex or ambiguous instructions.

## Limitations

The study used a between-subjects design where each participant heard only a single voice condition, precluding direct side-by-side comparisons of different avatars.
