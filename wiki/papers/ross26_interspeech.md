# Revisiting the NZE front vowel shift: evidence from New Zealand''s largest and most linguistically diverse city

[PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/ross26_interspeech.pdf) · [ISCA page](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2026/ross26_interspeech.html) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.21437/Interspeech.2026-1526)

**TL;DR** — Acoustic and statistical analysis of a century of speech in Auckland reveals a historical rise and modern fall of short front vowels, challenging standard accounts of the New Zealand English Short Front Vowel Shift.

## Problem

Auckland, New Zealand's largest and most linguistically diverse city, has been largely ignored in traditional sociophonetic narratives of New Zealand English (NZE) that historically focused on rural or South Island regions. This omission leaves a gap in understanding whether urban centers like Auckland acted as crucibles of linguistic innovation. Verifying historical phonetic descriptions is critical to resolving debates about the trajectory of NZE's Short Front Vowel Shift (SFVS).

## Method

The authors examine real- and apparent-time acoustic data across three speaker cohorts: historical speakers born 1896–1914 (8 speakers, recorded 1980–2002) and modern Auckland Voices speakers split into younger under-25 and older 40+ groups (67 speakers, recorded 2017–2018). Speech data was transcribed, forced-aligned via webMAUS, and subjected to formant extraction (F1, F2) using EmuR and forest. Linear mixed-effects models were fitted with gender-normalized bark-transformed formants to evaluate age-group effects and isolate phonological conditioning by adjacent laterals on the /ɪ/ vowel.

## Results

Linear mixed-effects models show significant F1 and F2 shifts for /e/ and /æ/, where historical speakers (F1: +0.93 bark for /e/, +0.63 for /æ/) and younger modern speakers (F1: +0.70 for /e/, +0.84 for /æ/) exhibit lowered and retracted centroids relative to the older modern reference group. While /ɪ/ showed overall stability, historical speakers demonstrated phonetic conditioning where adjacent laterals caused notable F2 retraction (higher F2 means with laterals), whereas modern speakers showed negligible lateral impact. These findings suggest historical /ɪ/ centralisation may have originated allophonically rather than strictly via push-chain pressure from /e/ and /æ/ raising.

## Limitations
